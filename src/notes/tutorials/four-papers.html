Four methods, and what they could become together

SuperMimic, SkipWorld, EfficientTDMPC and FlowDPG, explained in detail; then twenty ways to combine and extend them for a tracker that comes back from large perturbations.

How to read this

Part two, sections 6 to 8, is about our tracker: what it is trying to do, twenty ideas drawn from the four, and the five changes recommended for the plan. The map

The four papers answer four different questions, but they share one shape: a learned model of what happens next, a way to choose actions with it, and a discipline for keeping the model's errors from steering the choice. SuperMimic learns a humanoid's dynamics once and trains a tracking policy by differentiating through it. SkipWorld makes a model's long rollouts cheap by distilling a model that leaps several steps at once, then uses the cheap model to filter and the accurate one to decide. EfficientTDMPC keeps a planner honest by scoring an action sequence at every depth, averaging over an ensemble of models, and refusing to learn from actions the models are unsure about. FlowDPG teaches a generative policy to move toward higher value without differentiating through its own sampling, by distilling a value-driven correction into it at the policy's own estimate of its action.

1. SuperMimic: one differentiable world model

SuperMimic trains one whole-body controller for a humanoid across a large library of motion clips. The idea that makes it cheap is that every clip, however different, runs on the same body and the same contacts: one learned model of those dynamics can serve every clip. The policy is then improved not by sampling the simulator, as PPO does, but by differentiating an imagined return through the model, which gives the policy an analytic gradient with far less variance than a likelihood-ratio estimate.

The loop

The model

The model has a learned dynamics core and four heads, each either learned or computed analytically from the predicted state:

   s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t, xi)        the dynamics (learned)
   r_{t+1} = r(s_t, a_t, g_t)       the reward (analytic: the tracking reward from the predicted state)
   o_t     = g(s_t, xi)             the observation (analytic: what the policy reads, from the state)
   d_t     = d(s_t, g_t)            the termination probability (learned: a number in [0, 1])

   xi      the episode's simulation context: friction, calibration offsets, the torso's centre of mass

Two choices here matter more than they look. First, the simulator's randomness has known sources, so the model is deterministic and simply conditioned on the context xi; sensor noise is added outside the model when the policy acts in imagination. Second, an analytic head has no loss term and no error of its own: the reward the policy is optimized for is the true reward of the predicted state, not a second network's guess at it.

The model is fit on short windows from the replay. A window starts at a logged state and is rolled forward on the logged actions for k = 8 steps, each step from the model's own previous prediction, and every step is compared with what the simulator did:

   logged:     s_0 --a_0--> s_1 --a_1--> s_2 ... s_8
   the model:  s_0 --a_0--> s^_1 --a_1--> s^_2 ... s^_8        (each from the model's previous output)

   L_WM = sum over j = 1..8 of  |s^_j - s_j|^2  +  (r^_j - r_j)^2  +  |o^_j - o_j|^2  +  BCE(d^_j, d_j)
                                                 (no term for an analytic head)

Because the window is autoregressive, the model learns to correct its own drift. That is why it stays stable over rollouts far longer than eight steps: the paper's imagined horizon is 32, four times the fitting window, and the model holds up over indefinitely long rollouts in practice.

The policy, through the model

With the model frozen, the policy and a critic are trained on imagined rollouts of H = 32 steps. Each rollout starts from a replay state with its recorded context. The policy acts on the predicted observation plus re-sampled sensor noise. The objective is a discounted sum of predicted rewards, weighted by a survival weight, plus the critic's estimate of everything beyond the horizon:

   c_0 = 1,   c_{t+1} = c_t (1 - d^_{t+1})                          the survival weight: how likely the rollout is still alive

   J(theta) = E[ sum_{t=0}^{H-1} gamma^t c_t r^_{t+1}  +  gamma^H c_H V(s^_H, g_H, xi) ]

   gradient: backpropagation through time, through the model's Jacobians in s^ and a, over all H steps

The survival weight is SuperMimic's answer to termination. A simulator terminates an episode abruptly, which no gradient can pass through; the model instead predicts a probability of termination, and the weight multiplies every later reward by the probability of having survived so far. Falling costs the policy all the future rewards it would have collected, smoothly, so the gradient tells it in which direction falling becomes less likely.

The critic is fit to generalized-advantage returns computed backward over the imagined transitions, starting from its own estimate at the horizon, with no target network:

   R^_H = V_H
   R^_t = r^_{t+1} + gamma (1 - d^_{t+1}) [ (1 - lambda) V_{t+1} + lambda R^_{t+1} ]        t = H-1 ... 0

   L_V = sum_t (V_t - R^_t)^2,   the targets fixed while the critic is fit

Together, the policy and critic are a short-horizon actor-critic in the spirit of SHAC, which differentiates through a simulator; SuperMimic differentiates through a learned surrogate instead, which costs a model error but gains a rollout more than ten times faster than the simulator and a gradient the simulator never had.

2. SkipWorld: leap, then refine

SkipWorld plans visual navigation with a world model: given the current frames and a goal image, it rolls many candidate action sequences forward in the model and keeps the one whose predicted final view best matches the goal. Two things make this hard. Models trained on ground-truth histories are deployed in a closed loop where they see only their own predictions, so their errors compound. And scoring a candidate means several sequential model calls, so the cost grows with the horizon times the number of candidates.

Latent AutoRoll: train on your own predictions

   the context C_t = [z_{t-3}, z_{t-2}, z_{t-1}, z_t]      the latest latents the model conditions on

   step 1:  z^_{t+1} = F(C_t, a_{t+1}, tau)                 predict; loss against the true z_{t+1}
            C_{t+1} = shift(C_t, z^_{t+1})                   drop the oldest, append the PREDICTION, not the truth
   step 2:  z^_{t+2} = F(C_{t+1}, a_{t+2}, tau)             the model now sees its own output in its context
   ...
   L_AR = (1/H) sum_h L_diff(z_{t+h} | C_{t+h-1}, a_{t+h})

This is the same remedy SuperMimic applies with its autoregressive windows: the model trained on its own predictions learns to recover from its own errors, so what it does in a closed loop is what it was trained to do.

SkipDistill: a student that leaps

The dense model predicts one step at a time. The skip student predicts the endpoint of s steps in one call, from the current context, the actions over those steps added together, and the stride itself as a condition. Its target is not the ground truth, which would be the obvious choice. It is the dense teacher's own endpoint after s dense steps, with the teacher frozen:

Distilling from the teacher rather than the truth is the subtle choice. The student's job is to predict where the teacher would end up, because the planner's refinement stage uses the teacher: a student that agreed with the truth but not with the teacher would filter candidates the teacher then disagrees about.

Skip-then-Refine: the funnel

   N = 120 candidate action sequences over H = 8 steps
     |
     |  the student, stride 8: ONE call per candidate, the endpoint decoded and compared with the goal
     v
   P = 20 candidates with the lowest coarse cost
     |
     |  the teacher, stride 1: 8 dense calls per candidate, R = 3 stochastic rollouts each, costs averaged
     v
   top K candidates, their actions averaged into the action taken

   dense planning of all 120:    120 x 8 x 3 = 2880 dense calls
   skip-then-refine:             120 (student) + 20 x 8 x 3 = 600 teacher calls      2.7x faster
   accuracy: ATE 4.2% lower, RPE 2.7% lower than the dense baseline

The funnel is the point: the cheap model decides who is worth the expensive model's time, and the expensive model makes the decision. The ablation separates the two gains. AutoRoll alone brings the accuracy, ATE 3.26 against the baseline's 3.33, but as a dense planner it costs 3,808 seconds per query; the skip student alone is fast, 465 seconds, but less reliable on its own; AutoRoll with refinement reaches 3.20; the full funnel keeps that accuracy, 3.19, at 835 seconds. A larger stride loses almost nothing: stride 8 sits at near-peak accuracy with the best runtime.

3. EfficientTDMPC: an honest planner

The TD-MPC family learns a latent dynamics model, a reward head, a value ensemble and a policy, and plans with MPPI: it searches action sequences that maximize an estimated return, the predicted rewards over the sequence plus the value at its end. The estimate is built from learned parts, each of which errs, and a planner is exactly the thing that finds and exploits such errors: a sequence that the model wrongly believes leads to great reward wins the search. BMPC, the baseline, trains the policy entirely by distillation from the planner and refreshes stale targets by replanning on replay states, which deepens the dependence on the estimate. EfficientTDMPC's three changes each make the estimate harder to fool.

Value-equivalent learning

One background idea is needed first. The reward and value heads are trained on the latents the dynamics model rolls out, not on encoded true states. So they learn to compensate for the dynamics' errors: if the model drifts, the reward head is trained to predict the true reward from the drifted latent. This is the MuZero idea, and it couples the heads to the model.

The multi-horizon objective

A planned sequence is usually scored once, at its full depth. EfficientTDMPC scores it at every depth and averages:

   Q_h(z_t, a_t..a_{t+h-1}) = sum_{u<h} gamma^u R(z^_{t+u}, a_{t+u}) + gamma^h V(z^_{t+h})      the first h actions, then the critic

   Q_aggregate = (1/H) sum_{h=1}^{H} Q_h

   a sequence whose every prefix stays valuable under the policy's continuation wins;
   one whose value appears only at the last step does not

A value-equivalent ensemble

Several dynamics heads, four by default, are initialized differently and trained on the same windows; the reward and value heads are trained on every head's rollouts. A return estimate then rolls out with one dynamics head i and bootstraps with one value head j; the mean over all pairs is the return, and their variance is how much the models disagree:

   Q(i, j) = sum_u gamma^u R(z^_u) + gamma^H V_j(z^_H),   z^ rolled by dynamics head i
   Q_mean  = mean over (i, j)
   sigma^2 = variance over (i, j) / (N_f N_v)

   heads added to BMPC:   2 heads +10% AUC,   4 heads +22%,   8 heads +33%

Pessimistic reanalyze

When policy targets are refreshed by replanning on replay states, the planned actions are never executed, so the model is never corrected on them. The pessimistic objective subtracts the uncertainty:

   J_reanalyze = Q_mean - beta sigma_Q          beta = 10

   only actions the models agree about are distilled into the policy, which then proposes actions the models
   have been trained on - and the value targets built from them become reliable in turn

The paper's empirical rule is precise: pessimism helps on tasks that can terminate early, such as humanoid walking, sitting and balancing a pole, and does nothing or slightly hurts on tasks that cannot. A model's optimistic error on a terminating task is a fall it did not foresee.

The practical changes

  • Every transition enters the replay immediately, not at an episode's end, so new experience reaches the model, critic and policy without delay.
  • A longer planning horizon at acting time, together with the multi-horizon objective.
  • Reanalyze, which dominates wall-clock time, runs with one eighth of the planning budget: fewer particles, elites and policy seeds suffice when the planner only refreshes targets.

Together, on top of BMPC: the best sample efficiency reported on HumanoidBench and the DeepMind Control Suite, reaching BMPC's final performance in 57% fewer environment steps, without a significant increase in wall-clock time.

4. FlowDPG: improve a generative policy without differentiating through it

A flow-matching policy produces an action by integrating a learned velocity field from noise to a clean action chunk over a pseudo-time t from 0 to 1, in several steps. Such policies represent multimodal, feasible behaviour well, which is why manipulation has adopted them. But the standard way to improve a deterministic actor with a critic, DDPG's chain rule from the critic's gradient back to the actor's parameters, would have to pass through every integration step: a chain of Jacobians as long as the solver, expensive and numerically fragile, worst exactly when the velocity field is still poor.

The one-step estimate

From any point along the integration, the clean action can be estimated in one forward pass:

   x_t = (1 - t) x_0 + t x_1                       the noisy action at pseudo-time t (x_0 noise, x_1 the clean action)
   u_t = x_1 - x_0                                 the flow-matching target velocity

   a^ = x_t + (1 - t) v(x_t, t, s)                 the one-step estimate of the clean action, no gradient
   consistency: L_cons = |a^ - x_1|^2 = (1 - t)^2 |v - u_t|^2     keeps the estimate on the action manifold

The two vectors

   L = lambda |v(x_t, t, s) - u*_t|^2  +  (1 - lambda) |v(x_t, t, s) - u_t|^2
         the distilled, value-improved target           the behaviour-cloning anchor

   lambda warms up linearly from 0, so an unreliable early critic cannot dominate;
   the anchor is computed only on demonstrations and SUCCESSFUL rollouts, so it stays tied to behaviour that worked;
   inference is the unchanged multi-step ODE

Why it is a policy gradient

The vanilla deterministic policy gradient on the flow policy is an integral over pseudo-time of the critic's gradient times the sensitivity of the final action to the intermediate one, times the field's parameter gradient. FlowDPG's update equals it under three explicit approximations: the critic's gradient is taken at the one-step estimate, which sets the sensitivity to the identity; the integral is estimated at one random t, exactly as flow matching estimates its own loss; and the raw step size is replaced by the adaptive scaling. The last removes a known source of instability in the DDPG family.

Value learning that never leaves the data

The critics are twin Q networks whose minimum is used wherever a single estimate is needed. Their bootstrap target comes from an expectile value function, IQL's: a value trained toward the upper tail of the action-values of dataset actions, which never queries the critic at an action the data did not contain. Rewards per action chunk come from a frozen stage-and-progress predictor plus a terminal success term. The results on two real robots: 92% end-to-end success on dual-arm AirPods assembly over 8 stages, an 86% rubric score on scrambled-egg cooking over 14 stages, substantially above the alternatives that condition on value, bolt on an adapter, or differentiate through the ODE.

6. What our tracker is trying to do

Our system tracks a minute of captured dance with Geno, a 23-ball-joint character, on a GPU simulator that runs thousands of worlds at once, and it must do one thing the papers above do not set out to do: come back. A policy that tracks well in calm conditions is the easy half. The night's goal is a policy that resists pushes, recovers from stumbles, and returns to its reference after a large perturbation, in a way that looks natural. That makes termination the central problem: an episode that ends at the first loss teaches nothing about returning, and an episode that never ends teaches nothing about the cost of falling. Our episode rule already has a grace period, a loss, an aftermath in which the character may return, and a return streak; the curriculum starts episodes where the returns are low; the servo and the limbs' stiffness are part of the action.

The learning is SuperMimic's shape: a world model fit on short windows of logged physics, a policy improved by backpropagation through imagined rollouts, a critic beyond the horizon. What the other three papers add is precisely what a recovery policy needs. SkipWorld shows how to look further ahead cheaply. EfficientTDMPC shows how to keep a planner from believing a model that cannot know what a fall looks like. FlowDPG shows how to pull a policy toward higher value while anchoring it to behaviour known to work. The twenty ideas below are read with that in mind.

7. Twenty ways to combine and extend them

1. Soft termination from the judgement itself, no learned head

From SuperMimic's survival weight, with our analytic judgement. SuperMimic learns a termination probability because its simulator only reports a hard end. Our termination rule is a threshold on quantities the world model predicts: the tracking error, the head's height. So the survival weight can be analytic: a sigmoid of the margin to the threshold, d = sigmoid((error - threshold) / width), differentiable with respect to the predicted state, needing no training and never wrong about the rule. The width is the one parameter: a few centimetres of error. Cost: one function. Gain: the gradient of "do not fall" flows into the policy from the first round, from the true rule.

2. The imagined return scored at every depth

EfficientTDMPC's multi-horizon objective, applied to SuperMimic's BPTT. The policy's imagined objective bootstraps the critic once, at the horizon. Average instead the returns bootstrapped at depths 1 to H, or weight them as a lambda return. A policy is then rewarded for rollouts that stay good all along, which a model error rarely fabricates at every depth at once, and the gradient's variance from the far steps, where the model is least sure, falls. Cost: one loop over depths in the loss. Gain: stability of the policy's gradient at long imagination horizons, which recovery needs.

3. Two world-model heads, and their disagreement as a number we can use

EfficientTDMPC's value-equivalent ensemble, at the smallest size. Two dynamics heads, differently initialized, trained on the same windows, the critic trained on both heads' rollouts. The imagined return is averaged over the heads, and their disagreement along a rollout is an uncertainty the rest of the system can read: the planner (idea 9), the curriculum (idea 11), the diagnostics. Cost: twice the model's compute, a small share of a round. Gain: an uncertainty with no extra machinery, and a less exploitable model.

4. The reference as the anchor

FlowDPG's two vectors, read for a residual policy. Our action is an offset around the reference pose: the servo toward the reference is the feasibility vector, and the policy's offset is the value-driven correction. Make the anchor explicit: a term that pulls the offset toward zero, weighted by (1 - lambda), with lambda warming up from zero as the model and critic become trustworthy. Early in training the policy is the servo plus noise, which tracks reasonably; later the correction takes over where it earns value. Cost: one loss term and a schedule. Gain: no early collapse from a critic that does not yet know anything.

5. The physics-MPC teacher distilled the FlowDPG way

FlowDPG's adaptive shift and warm-up, for our teacher. The teacher finds, in the device physics, an offset that improves the imagined return. Distill it as FlowDPG distills its critic's correction: the target is the policy's own action plus a step along the teacher's direction, the step scaled to the action's natural size, delta = alpha |a| g / (|g| + eps), with the teacher's weight lambda warming up. The teacher never replaces the policy's action with its own; it bends it. Cost: the teacher's search, already planned. Gain: stable distillation, and a teacher that fades naturally as the policy's own correction grows.

6. The skip model distilled from the dense model's endpoint

SkipDistill. Our skip model, which predicts three decisions ahead in one leap, is best trained not only on logged physics but on the dense model's own three-step rollout, stop-gradient, with the actions over the leap aggregated and the stride as a condition. The two models then agree with each other, which is what matters when one screens and the other decides. Cost: three dense model steps per skip target, in the same batch. Gain: a skip model that predicts where the dense model would go, so a funnel built on them is consistent.

7. Skip-then-refine for the teacher

SkipWorld's funnel, for our MPC. The teacher's candidates are many; the physics is expensive. Score every candidate offset sequence with the skip model in one leap, keep the best few, and run only those in the physics. SkipWorld's numbers suggest a factor near three with no loss of accuracy. Cost: none beyond idea 6. Gain: a teacher the GPU can afford every decision, for every world in trouble.

8. Two teachers for the skip model

SkipDistill, with the physics we have. SkipWorld distills from the dense model because it has no ground truth in a closed loop. We have the physics: for logged action sequences, the skip model's endpoint can be regressed to the physics' true state three decisions later; for imagined sequences, to the dense model's endpoint. One loss, two teachers, by which data each term has. Cost: bookkeeping. Gain: the skip model is anchored to the truth where the truth exists.

9. A pessimistic teacher

EfficientTDMPC's pessimism, in the planner. The teacher scores a candidate by the imagined return minus beta times the heads' disagreement along its rollout. A candidate that looks wonderful to one head and dreadful to the other is a model error, and exactly the kind a planner finds. Cost: idea 3. Gain: the teacher proposes only what the model can vouch for.

10. Reanalyze the teacher on replay states

BMPC's reanalyze, EfficientTDMPC's cheap budget. The teacher need not run only on live worlds. Between decisions, on states from the replay, a reduced search refreshes the teacher's offsets as training targets, so the policy learns from the teacher's current opinion rather than a stale one. Cost: GPU time the lab can measure. Gain: many more teacher targets per hour.

11. Curriculum by disagreement

SuperMimic's curriculum, with EfficientTDMPC's uncertainty. Our starts are drawn by a mixture toward bins of low return. Add a term toward bins where the model's heads disagree most: starts land where the model needs data, which is also where the policy will later need to recover. Cost: one more mixture weight. Gain: the data goes where the model is weak before the policy exploits the weakness.

12. A generative head for the aftermath

FlowDPG's flow policy, for recovery only. Tracking is unimodal; recovery is not: which foot steps, which way the arms swing. A small flow-matching head for the aftermath, improved FlowDPG-style from the critic's and teacher's corrections, with the deterministic head kept for tracking. The simplest form is a mixture of a few recovery modes, the critic choosing. Cost: a second head and its sampler. Gain: recoveries that commit to one of several good strategies instead of averaging them into a bad one.

13. Action chunks in trouble

FlowDPG's chunks with SkipWorld's leap. In the aftermath, the policy proposes a chunk of three decisions at once, scored by the skip model in one leap: fewer, larger decisions while recovering, dense decisions while tracking. Cost: the policy's output widens for the chunk. Gain: recoveries planned as a whole motion rather than step by step.

14. The critic as SuperMimic fits it

Generalized-advantage returns computed backward over the imagined rollout, from the critic's own estimate at the horizon, with the survival weight in the recursion and no target network. Cost: none. Gain: one fewer network to keep in step, and a critic trained on exactly the returns the policy optimizes.

15. Noise outside the model

SuperMimic. Sensor noise is re-sampled and added to the predicted observation when the policy acts in imagination, never modelled. The policy learns to be robust to it; the model stays deterministic. Cost: none. Gain: robustness without a noise model, and a cleaner model to fit.

16. The simulation context as a model input

SuperMimic's xi. When randomization comes, friction and mass enter the model as inputs rather than as unexplained variation, and the policy can read them too. Cost: a few inputs. Gain: one model for every randomized world; the way to real-robot transfer later.

17. Goal interfaces sharing one model

SuperMimic's goal encoders. Beside the reference-tracking goal, a velocity or direction goal trained through the same model, so Geno can later be steered rather than only replayed. Cost: an encoder and a reward. Gain: a controllable character from the same training.

18. One network, two strides

Our skip model, simplified by SkipWorld. Rather than a second network, the world model takes the stride as an input and predicts the mean rate over it; dense windows and skip windows train the same weights in one batch. Cost: one input. Gain: no second model to keep consistent, and shared features between the scales.

19. Pessimism only where episodes can end

EfficientTDMPC's rule, applied within one task. Pessimism helps where termination is possible and hurts where it is not. For us both happen in the same run: calm tracking rarely ends, the aftermath often does. Apply the disagreement penalty only to worlds in trouble, in the teacher and in the imagined return. Cost: a flag we already have. Gain: caution where it pays, speed where it does not.

20. The model's error as the gate for everything

SuperMimic's scaling plot, as a discipline. Every learning signal above rides on the model. The model's short-window error against the physics, on live rollouts, is the one number that says whether any of them can be trusted: the lab reports it per experiment, the teacher's weight and the imagination horizon follow it, and a night whose model error climbs stops its policy updates before they do harm. Cost: a measurement we already planned. Gain: the whole system fails safe.

9. Far from the reference: a wild brainstorm

SuperMimic's policy is trained near its references: episodes start on a clip frame, the action noise is small, and a lost reference ends the episode. That is enough to track, and not enough to come back. Everything below asks one question: what would it take for the same recipe to learn to return from metres away, from the ground, from the middle of a fall, and to make the return look like the dance it belongs to? The ideas are deliberately wide. Some are small and sure; some would be research. Each says which paper's tool it borrows.

A. Where episodes start

A1. A curriculum of distance, not only of return

Start noise today is a few centimetres and a small velocity. Make the start a distribution over distance to the reference, drawn by the curriculum: a share of starts on the frame, a share pushed a metre off, a share lying on the ground near the frame's position, a share dropped from a height. SuperMimic's mixture then runs over start kinds as well as bins: the kinds whose returns are lowest get more practice, with a uniform floor so none is abandoned. The curriculum already measures returns per bin; a return per kind is the same bookkeeping.

A2. The ground poses the physics gives us

Where should a character on the ground start? Not in a hand-written pose. Drop the character from every clip frame, let the physics settle it for a second, and keep the resting poses: a library of ground states the body actually reaches, each labelled with the frame it fell from. Starts on the ground draw from this library, with the clip continuing from that frame. The library costs one physics pass over the clips, on the device, and the poses are natural by construction.

A3. Starts in mid-fall

Between upright and ground lies the fall itself: the states where a recovery is still possible but closing. Record the falling states from pushed episodes - the aftermath's first second - into a replay of their own, and start a share of episodes from them. These are the states where one good decision changes the outcome, and they are rare in ordinary rollouts. EfficientTDMPC's practical lesson applies: insert them the moment they happen.

A4. Hindsight starts: resume where a recovery was lost

When an aftermath ends in failure, the states a few decisions before the end are the ones to practise. Start new episodes there, with the same clip and frame, so the policy meets the same hard moment again from slightly earlier, until it passes. This is a curriculum at the level of single states rather than bins, and the replay already holds every state needed.

A5. Pushes that scale with competence

A push schedule that opens with training is in the plan. Make it close the loop: the push rate and speed per bin follow that bin's return estimate, so the policy is pushed hardest where it tracks best and gently where it is still learning. The same mixture arithmetic as the curriculum, read the other way round.

B. What the policy is paid for

B1. Pay for approach, not only for arrival

Far from the reference, the tracking reward is nearly zero everywhere, so its gradient says nothing. Add a potential: a reward proportional to the decrease of distance to the reference per decision - position, heading and pose separately - which is dense at any distance and vanishes exactly when the character is back. A potential-based term leaves the optimal policy unchanged near the reference and gives the far policy a direction. It is analytic, differentiable through the model, and SuperMimic's objective takes it as one more term.

B2. The soft survival weight shaped by distance

Idea 1 of the previous section makes termination a sigmoid of the error margin. With far starts, no single threshold fits: a character a metre off has not "lost" what it never had. Let the threshold follow the start: an episode that began far is allowed to be far, and its threshold tightens as it approaches, like a leash shortening. The survival weight then means "the character is not getting further away than it has been", which is the right thing to never do.

B3. A return bonus with a clock

SuperMimic has no reward for returning, only for being there. Give the aftermath a bonus at the moment the return streak completes, discounted by how long it took: a fast return earns more than a slow one. The bonus is a single step's reward, which the critic learns to anticipate, and it makes the policy prefer the decisive recovery over the cautious one that takes four seconds.

B4. Naturalness as closeness to the captures, not to the frame

A return should look like the dance. Reward the pose's distance to the nearest captured pose over all clips - not the reference frame's pose, which may be far - so that even a character on the ground moves through poses the captures contain. The rotation boxes already describe the captures' envelope; a soft version of their excess is this reward's cheapest form. Where the captures hold no get-up, this term still prefers a return that passes through captured poses to one that does not.

B5. The multi-horizon objective as a brake on wishful recoveries

EfficientTDMPC's averaging over depths matters most far from the data. A recovery the model invents that pays only at step 32 is exactly a model error; a recovery that lowers the distance at every depth is real. The averaged objective weights the near steps, where the model is good, and lets the far steps vote without deciding.

C. A model that knows what it does not know

C1. Two heads far from home

The model has seen little of the ground, the mid-fall, the metre-off state. Two heads trained on the same data disagree there, and the disagreement is a map of where the model is lost. Use it three ways: subtract it from the imagined return in the aftermath (pessimism, EfficientTDMPC); send starts there (curriculum by disagreement); and shorten the imagination horizon where it is large, since a model that is lost at step 5 should not be trusted at step 32.

C2. The physics as the model's teacher where it is lost

Where the heads disagree, run the physics: a few decisions from that state, with the policy's actions, logged straight into the replay with high priority. The device simulator is cheap and exact; the model is cheap and approximate; the disagreement decides which one to spend. This is SkipWorld's teacher-and-student with the roles assigned by uncertainty.

C3. A model fit on recoveries as a second window

The model's 8-step windows come from everywhere in the replay, mostly calm tracking. Keep a second sampler that draws windows from the aftermath only, in proportion to how badly the model does there, so the dynamics of stumbling, catching, and standing up get the share of the fit they need. Data freshness, as EfficientTDMPC insists, but by region of state space.

C4. The skip model as the long-range eye

A recovery is decided over seconds, not decisions. A skip model that leaps a second predicts, from a far state, roughly where the character will be after one second of a given intent, which is all a recovery plan needs at its coarse level. SkipWorld's student is trained exactly for this: the endpoint, from the dense model's rollout, with the stride as input. For recovery, its error on the ground states is what to watch.

D. Planning the way back

D1. The funnel for recoveries only

The physics-MPC teacher is expensive; recoveries are rare. Run the teacher only for worlds in the aftermath: the skip model screens many candidate intents over a second, the physics refines the few, and the chosen offset is distilled FlowDPG-style. Calm tracking never pays for the teacher. SkipWorld's 2.7 times becomes larger here, because most worlds need no teacher at all.

D2. Plan in intents, not in joints

A recovery's candidates should not be random joint offsets; they should be a few intents: step left, step right, crouch, reach a hand down, roll. Each intent is a short captured or hand-made motion played through the servo, and the planner chooses among intents and their timings. The candidate set is tiny, the physics refines it, and the distilled policy learns which intent the state calls for. SkipWorld's action aggregation over a stride is the same thing: the sum of actions over the leap is an intent.

D3. Re-anchor the goal: the frame that is easiest to return to

A character on the ground should not chase the frame the clock says; it should rejoin the dance at the frame it can reach soonest. Let the planner choose the target frame among the next few seconds of the clip, scored by the imagined cost of getting there, and let the reference's clock jump to it on return. The reference becomes a path to rejoin rather than a schedule to keep, which is what a dancer who stumbles actually does.

D4. Time-reversed falls as get-up references

The captures hold no get-up, but the physics holds thousands of falls. Reverse a recorded fall in time and it is a get-up: physically implausible in its forces, but a plausible path of poses from the ground to standing. Use reversed falls as references for the servo during a get-up, with the policy's offsets correcting what the reversal gets wrong. Then the time-reversed fall library is a free, natural, body-specific set of get-up motions.

D5. Reanalyze the hard moments

BMPC replans on replay states to refresh policy targets. Do it only for the hardest states: the mid-fall and ground states with the lowest returns, a reduced-budget teacher run between decisions. The policy's targets at the rare, decisive moments stay fresh without the cost of replanning everywhere.

E. What the policy is

E1. One policy, two conditions

The simplest change: give the policy its distance to the reference and the aftermath flag as inputs. A network that knows it is far behaves differently from one that thinks it is near, and the same weights serve both. Without this, the policy must infer from the pose alone that it is on the ground.

E2. A flow-matching head for the first second of trouble

The first second after a big push is where strategies diverge - the foot that steps, the side that bends - and a deterministic policy averages them into a crouch. A small flow policy proposes the first second's intent, improved FlowDPG-style from the critic's and the teacher's corrections, anchored to recoveries that succeeded; the tracker takes over once the intent is under way. Multimodality where it matters, determinism everywhere else.

E3. Action chunks that the skip model scores

In trouble, the policy emits a chunk of three decisions; the skip model scores the chunk in one leap; the chunk runs. Fewer decisions while recovering, each with a view a second ahead, and the dense policy resumes when the reference is near. FlowDPG's chunks with SkipWorld's stride.

E4. Stiffness as the recovery's dial

The limbs' stiffness is already part of the action. A fall wants soft limbs on impact and stiff ones when pushing off; let the policy learn that dial under the recovery rewards, and let the teacher's intents carry a stiffness profile of their own. This is the one lever a human recovering also uses, and no paper's policy has it.

F. The data

F1. Relabel every recovery with the frame it reached

A recovery that rejoined the dance at frame 1,240 was, in hindsight, a successful episode toward frame 1,240 whatever the clock said. Store it with that goal, as hindsight relabelling does, so the replay fills with successful returns from far states toward reachable frames. The model learns the same dynamics either way; the policy learns from successes it did not plan.

F2. Successful recoveries as the anchor

FlowDPG computes its behaviour-cloning anchor only on demonstrations and successful rollouts. Do the same: the policy's anchor far from the reference is not the servo toward a frame it cannot reach, but its own successful recoveries from similar states. The anchor set grows as the policy improves, and the policy is pulled toward what has worked.

F3. Record the pushes, learn to predict them

The model sees a push as an unexplained jump. Log the push as an input to the model - an external impulse for one decision - so it is predicted rather than absorbed as error, and so the policy can be trained in imagination against pushes the model applies itself. SuperMimic's sim context, extended to the one thing that changes within an episode.

G. Two wild ones

G1. A model of the recovery itself

Train the skip model to predict not the state after one second but the outcome: the distance to the reference after one second under an intent, and whether the character is still upright. That is a value function of intents, learned from physics outcomes, usable by the planner with no rollout at all. It is EfficientTDMPC's value-equivalence pushed to its end: the model that predicts only what the decision needs.

G2. Train the fall

Every fall the policy has is data; a policy that falls well - lands softly, ends in a pose the get-up library knows - makes the recovery that follows possible. Give the fall its own small reward, the landing's impact and the resting pose's distance to the library, so the policy learns to fall toward states it can get up from. Recovery then starts before the ground.

What I would try first

Four of these are cheap enough and promising enough for the experiment that trains the new signals together, and they compose: a distance curriculum with ground starts from the physics' own resting poses (A1, A2); the approach potential and the leash (B1, B2), both analytic and differentiable; the policy told its distance and its aftermath (E1); and the funnel for recoveries only (D1), so the teacher's cost is paid where it buys something. The re-anchored goal (D3) is the one idea here that could change what a tracker is, and it costs a search over a few frames with the imagined cost we already compute; it deserves an experiment of its own on the second night.

10. Four ideas worked through

The brainstorm above is wide on purpose. This part narrows: the four ideas that could change what the tracker is, each written precisely enough to build, with a worked example, its cost in a round, how it fails, and how one hour of an experiment would tell.

10.1 The approach potential and the leash

The rule. Let D be a distance from the character to its reference, built from the same quantities the judgement already computes: the root's horizontal offset, its heading error, and the mean joint error, each divided by a scale so that one unit of each is "clearly lost". Two terms enter the imagined objective:

   approach reward at step t:   r_approach = k_a (D_{t-1} - D_t)          dense at any distance; zero once D stops changing
   the leash:                   L_t = min(L_{t-1}, D_t + margin)          never longer than it has been
   soft termination:            d_t = sigmoid((D_t - L_t) / width)        "getting further away than you have been"
   survival weight:             c_{t+1} = c_t (1 - d_{t+1})               as SuperMimic's

The potential is telescoping: over a whole episode its sum is k_a (D_start - D_end), so it does not change which recovery is best, only how early the policy is told that it is on the right track. The leash starts at the start's own distance plus a margin, so an episode that began a metre off is allowed a metre, and it shortens as the character closes in. Near the reference, the leash equals the ordinary threshold and this is exactly idea 1 of part 7.

A worked example. A start 1.0 m off, heading right, pose on the frame; scales of 0.3 m, 0.5 rad and 0.2 rad; a margin of 0.5 units; a width of 0.3 units; ka = 2.

   decision     D      leash L   d (fall)   r_approach   r_tracking   note
   start       3.3     3.8       0.00       -            0.0          a metre off is 3.3 units; the leash allows 3.8
   1           3.1     3.6       0.00       +0.4         0.0          moving: paid for approach, nothing for tracking
   2           2.6     3.1       0.00       +1.0         0.0
   3           2.8     3.1       0.27       -0.4         0.0          a stumble: D rises, d rises, c falls to 0.73
   4           2.1     2.6       0.00       +1.4         0.1          recovered: the leash tightens again
   8           0.6     1.1       0.00       +0.6         0.6          the tracking reward takes over
   12          0.2     0.7       0.00       +0.1         0.9          home; the leash is the ordinary threshold

At decisions 1 and 2 the tracking reward is zero and the approach reward is the only signal; by decision 8 the roles have swapped. The stumble at decision 3 costs 27% of the remaining rewards through the survival weight, which is the gradient saying "do not give ground".

Cost. Three numbers per world per decision, computed from the judgement's own quantities; nothing learned. Failure modes. A potential with too large ka rewards rushing: the policy dives toward the reference and arrives falling. The telescoping bounds the total, but the per-step shaping can still dominate the tracking reward midway; the scales must put "clearly lost" at one unit and ka near the tracking reward's size. A leash with no margin terminates on noise. The hour. Starts at 0.5 and 1.0 m on the control's clips; the share that returns within four seconds, against the control's, at 20, 40 and 60 minutes.

10.2 Re-anchoring the goal

The rule. In the aftermath, every decision, consider the clip's frames from the clock's frame to a few seconds ahead, at a coarse step. For each candidate frame, estimate the cost of reaching it: the distance D to that frame now, divided by a speed the character can manage, plus the imagined return of heading there if the skip model is at hand, minus the tracking reward forgone by skipping the frames between. Choose the cheapest. The reference's clock jumps to the chosen frame at the moment of return, and until then the goal the policy sees is the chosen frame, with the clip continuing from it.

   candidates:   f = clock, clock + 0.5 s, clock + 1.0 s, ..., clock + 3 s          (seven frames)
   cost(f)   =   D(now, f) / speed  +  skipped(f) x penalty_per_second  -  imagined_return(f) x weight
   goal      =   argmin cost; the clock jumps to goal on return (return streak complete)

Cost. Seven distances per world in the aftermath per decision, each a judgement-sized computation; with the skip model, seven leaps. Nothing for worlds that are tracking. Failure modes. A goal that jumps every decision is a goal the policy cannot aim at: the choice must be sticky, changing only when a candidate beats the current goal by a clear margin. A penalty per skipped second that is too small lets the character "rejoin" by skipping to a frame it happens to resemble, learning nothing of the dance between; too large and this is the ordinary tracker. The records must store which frame was the goal, since the policy's observation changes with it. The hour. Pushes of 1.5 m/s on the control's clips, the goal re-anchored against the goal on the clock; the returns within four seconds, and - the measure this idea exists for - how natural the returns look in the evaluation's pictures.

10.3 Planning in intents

The rule. An intent is a short motion played through the servo for one second: a step with the left foot, with the right, a crouch, a hand reaching to the floor, a roll, each as a few key poses interpolated, plus "do nothing", which is the tracker. The teacher's candidate set in the aftermath is the intents times a few timings, twenty or so candidates. The skip model scores each by the imagined distance and survival after a second; the physics refines the best three; the winning intent's first decision becomes the teacher's offset, distilled as in idea 5.

   candidates:   { step L, step R, crouch, reach down, roll, hold }  x  { now, in 1 decision, in 2 }   = 18
   screen:       18 skip leaps  ->  imagined D and survival after one second
   refine:       the best 3 in the physics, one second each
   distill:      the winner's first offset, scaled to the policy's step, with the warm-up

Why intents and not offsets. Random joint offsets over a second span a space the physics will not reward in any direction most of the time; a planner finds nothing. Six intents span the space of what a body does when it stumbles, and the physics ranks them reliably. SkipWorld's candidates are random action sequences because its action is two numbers; ours is seventy-five.

Cost. Per world in the aftermath per decision: 18 leaps of the skip model and three seconds of physics - against the thousands of worlds tracking, which pay nothing. If a tenth of the worlds are in trouble, the teacher costs about a third of a round's physics. Failure modes. Intents are hand-made, so a recovery that needs a motion no intent contains is not found; the policy can still discover it under the rewards, but not from the teacher. An intent library that grows with the captures' own recoveries, once some succeed, is the natural repair. The hour. The teacher with intents against the teacher with random offsets, both distilled the same way; the share of returns, and the distilled policy's returns with the teacher switched off at the end of the hour.

10.4 Time-reversed falls

The rule. From every pushed episode that ends on the ground, keep the last two seconds of the fall as a clip of poses; reverse it in time; smooth the velocities; store it, labelled with the resting pose it starts from and the standing frame it ends at. When an episode starts on the ground, the servo's reference for the first two seconds is the reversed fall whose resting pose is nearest, and the goal then re-anchors to the clip. The policy's offsets and the physics correct what the reversal gets wrong: a reversed fall has the right poses in the right order and the wrong forces, and the servo supplies forces of its own.

Cost. The clip storage of a few hundred two-second motions, and a nearest-pose lookup at ground starts. Failure modes. A reversed fall begins with the body pushing the floor as it never pushed during the fall; the servo may fail to lift the torso in time and the reversed reference runs ahead. A slower playback rate for the first second, or re-anchoring the goal to a later point of the reversed clip when the body lags, keeps the reference within reach. Falls that end face-down reverse into get-ups that start face-down, which is right; but a fall that ended tangled gives a get-up no body performs - the library should keep only falls whose resting pose the rotation boxes accept. The hour. Ground starts with the reversed references against ground starts with the clip's frame as reference; the share standing within three seconds.

The round's budget with all four

Taking the control's round as one unit of physics for its worlds, the four add: the leash and potential, nothing; re-anchoring, seven distance computations for the worlds in the aftermath; intents, eighteen leaps and three seconds of physics for those worlds; the reversed library, a lookup at ground starts. With a tenth of the worlds in trouble and two model heads, the round costs about one and a half units: the control's physics, a third more for the teacher's refinements, and the model's second head on the learning side. Everything else is bookkeeping the device already does.

11. The design decided: a planner-fed flow offset on a SuperMimic tracker

This section states one architecture precisely, as decided on 7 November: SuperMimic's tracker as the base, a flow-matching offset on top of it learned the FlowDPG way, a planner in the manner of EfficientTDMPC that screens with the skip model and refines in the real physics, and the planner's executed actions feeding every learner.

The pieces

   W     the world model: dense, SuperMimic's, fit on 8-step windows of EVERY trajectory; analytic reward,
         observation and termination; two heads (their disagreement is the planner's uncertainty)
   S     the skip model: stride k = 3 decisions, one network with the stride as input, distilled from W's
         endpoint and from the physics' true endpoint where logged actions exist
   pi    the base policy: deterministic, SuperMimic's residual tracker
   F     the offset policy: a flow-matching head; it sees the observation, the goal, pi's action, the distance to the
         reference and the aftermath flag; it emits ONE decision's offset delta
   T     the planner: EfficientTDMPC's objective in our setting, screened by S, refined in the physics
   the action executed in a policy world:   a = pi(o, g) + delta,  delta ~ F, one sample per decision, ALWAYS on
   the deployed controller, at evaluation and on the nights' runs:   pi + F alone; T is a training device

The planner T

   horizon H = 6 decisions (two leaps of S)
   candidates: 64 offset sequences - pi's action plus samples of F plus noise, the previous winner shifted one
               decision as the warm start (receding horizon)
   screen:     S scores all 64 in two leaps each: the imagined return at every depth, averaged (the multi-horizon
               objective), averaged over W's two heads, minus beta x their disagreement for a world in trouble
   refine:     the best 3 run in the PHYSICS for the 6 decisions; the best of the 3 wins
   execute:    the winner's FIRST offset only; then replan from what actually happened
   reward:     the tracking reward with SuperMimic's penalties, plus the leash and the approach potential

Two kinds of worlds, every decision

   policy worlds     execute pi + delta (delta ~ F)          ordinary experience, flagged "policy"
   planner worlds    execute T's first offset               real trajectories under planned actions, flagged "planned"
   which worlds plan: a fixed share of all worlds (a tenth), plus every world in trouble (the aftermath)

What each learner is fed

   W, S   every trajectory, both kinds - the planned ones correct W exactly where a planner's errors would hide
   pi     BPTT through W from replay states (SuperMimic), plus a distillation term toward the planned actions at
          planned states (BMPC-style, its weight a dial kept modest: where plans are multimodal, pi averages them and F
          carries the modes)
   F      FlowDPG: the anchor is the planned offsets at planned states, computed only on planned trajectories that
          SUCCEEDED (returned, or kept the reference); the correction g is the gradient of W's imagined return with
          respect to delta - analytic, a short BPTT - taken at F's one-step estimate of delta, scaled to the flow's
          own step, its weight lambda warming up from zero
   critic every trajectory; bootstraps T's returns and pi's imagined returns beyond the horizon

What stays open

The share of planner worlds, the distillation weight on pi, F's offset scale in calm tracking, beta, the leash's margin and width, and the skip model's accuracy on far states are all dials; the lab's screen is where they are set. The one risk the design accepts knowingly: pi is distilled from multimodal plans and will average them where they diverge, so F must be able to carry the whole of a recovery's mode by itself - which is what its anchor on successful plans is for.
