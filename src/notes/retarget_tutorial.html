1. The problem, precisely

We have a motion-capture take — a person dancing — recorded as a BVH file driving the Geno skeleton. We have a robot, humanoid.xml from the MuJoCo examples. We want the robot to perform the dance: for every frame of the capture, a joint configuration of the robot that reads as the same pose.

Three things make this harder than it sounds, and every failure in this project traces to one of them:

  1. The two skeletons are different shapes. Different bone lengths, different joint counts (96 versus 16 bodies), different numbers of degrees of freedom (a human elbow versus one hinge), different rest poses.
  2. The two live in different coordinate conventions. Y-up centimetres versus Z-up metres, facing different directions, with joint coordinates that mean different things.
  3. A robot body cannot be placed directly. Only its root is free; everything else reaches its pose through joint angles, and a hinge can only do so much.

A retarget is therefore not a copy. It is a translation between two languages that don't share a vocabulary — and the mistakes were, almost without exception, places where we assumed a shared word that wasn't there.

The row that cost the most

humanoid.xml's qpos0 is not a T-pose. Its arms fold up into a triangle. An early check compared "hand height ≈ shoulder height" and pronounced it a T-pose — but a folded arm satisfies that too. Drawing the two rest poses side by side settled in one glance what a carefully chosen scalar had confirmed wrongly. Every twist offset built before that point compared two different physical poses.

The row that cost the second most

The spine runs the opposite direction in the two hierarchies. "Root" and "hips" are the same body on Geno and opposite ends of a chain on the robot. Every piece of code that assumed they agree — the local-rotation reference, the chain ordering, the T-pose anchor — was wrong by the height of a spine.

3. Frames, units, conventions

Y-up to Z-up

position: (x, y, z)_Yup → (x, −z, y)_Zup quaternion: (w, vx, vy, vz)_Yup → (w, vx, −vz, vy)_Zup the imaginary part maps like a vector equivalently R q R⁻¹ with R = rotation of +90° about X

Both forms are equivalent; the vector form is cheaper and harder to get backwards. FlomoGMR documents exactly this map in flomo_to_geno_bvh.py.

Scale

scale = cm_to_m × (robot_hip_height / geno_hip_height) = 0.01 × (0.830 / 0.855) ≈ 0.0097

Two bugs lived here. A hardcoded 0.9 for the robot's hip height (it is 0.830) skewed every target by 8.5% for most of the project. And hip_height is stored in metres while rest_positions are in centimetres — applying the conversion to both drew a T-pose the size of a building. Two fields of one struct in different units is a trap; the unit belongs in the name.

Facing

Geno faces +Z; the robot faces +X. In the robot's frame that is a single yaw about Z. Measured empirically at −90°; the derivation gives +90°, and the discrepancy is recorded rather than smoothed over — one of the handedness conventions is opposite to the assumption. In the final recipe the facing is absorbed by the twist offsets and needs no separate constant.

4. The rest pose — the root of everything

Every retarget needs a reference: a pose in which the source and target are known to depict the same thing, so that motion can be measured as departure from it. The reference pose problem broke this project five times, each in a new costume:

#What was comparedWhat it should have beenSpace

The construction that finally holds

Don't hope the two rest poses match — build the robot's from the source's. solveRestPoseFromSource walks the robot's own tree, placing each body along the direction Geno's corresponding bone points, at the robot's own bone length. Every target is then exactly reachable, the shape is a T-pose by construction, and one translation anchors the pelvis (not the tree root) on Geno's hips.

for each body, parents first: target[body] = target[parent] + normalize(geno_bone) × |robot_bone| then shift all targets so target[pelvis] = geno_hips then IK the robot onto the targets (position only) then record qpos_tpose, and rest_flexion[j] for every 1-DOF hinge

5. The recipe we arrived at

Three steps, one sentence each

  1. Rest pose. Solve the robot into the capture's T-pose using the capture's directions and the robot's own bone lengths. Once.
  2. Per frame, parents first: for each body, decide its world orientation (twist-offset or aim), fit it onto the body's joints against the parent's achieved orientation, and for a 1-DOF hinge write wanted − rest.
  3. Refine. A few damped-least-squares steps on weighted position targets at the feet and hands.

The recipe is understandable. Nothing in it needs two coordinate conventions held in mind at once, and every step can be checked against a drawing: does the bone point the right way, does the elbow bend in the right plane, does the foot land.

The one genuinely open design question is which mechanism decides the per-body orientation in step 2. Two exist and both are wired:

MechanismWhat it doesStrengthWeakness
A — twist offsets
ported from GMR		Converts a rotation from the source frame to the robot's via a per-bone R_twist derived from the two rest poses; chains through inv(parent_twist).Carries twist. Wins 5 of 7 stages, legs by 2×.Every rest-pose bug in the project was this mechanism's.
B — aimingCompares directions in world space; rotates the bone onto the target.Structurally immune to rest-pose errors — a direction carries its own frame.Leaves twist free. Wins only torso and elbow bend.

Measured with everything else identical, A wins. Per-body mixing of the two is worse than either, because A's inv(parent_twist) undoes a twist B never applied.

6. The mathematics, step by step

6.1 Twist offsets (mechanism A) — the ported recipe

From FlomoGMR's compute_twist_offsets, with one adaptation.

Setup, once, from the two rest poses. For each body J with a child bone: d_robot = normalize(rest_xpos[child] − rest_xpos[J]) in the robot's LOCAL frame d_src = normalize(tpose[src_child] − tpose[src_J]) in world, converted to the robot's frame j_rot = the source joint's T-pose WORLD rotation, converted to the robot's frame d_target = j_rot⁻¹ · d_src the source direction, in J's local frame R_twist[J] = minimumAngleRotation(d_robot → d_target) Per frame, on LOCAL rotations, walking each chain parent to child: q_local[J] = conj(world_src[map[robot_parent(J)]]) · world_src[map[J]] want[J] = inv(R_twist[parent]) · q_local[J] · R_twist[J] Why it is exact: world_robot[J] = world_src[J] · R_twist[J] ⇒ world_robot[J] · d_robot = world_src[J] · d_target the bone direction matches by construction

Two adaptations the port needed, and why

The inv(parent) factor is the piece we did not have for many turns: it undoes the parent's twist so a chain does not accumulate it. Without it a correction that helps a shoulder wrecks the forearm below it — exactly the failure we saw.

q_local is taken relative to the human joint driving the robot's parent, not the human's own parent. That one expression handles both a skipped joint (the arm's RightShoulder maps to no robot body) and the inverted spine — and gives the root the human's world rotation as a consequence, since its robot parent is the world. Getting this wrong cost 51° at the torso.

The reference never needed either, because its Geno MJCF was generated from the BVH skeleton, so its robot's frames were the source's. A port is only faithful if its assumptions port too.

6.2 Aiming with chosen twist (mechanism B)

swing = shortestArc(robot_bone_local → target_dir) constrains 2 of 3 DOF the third — rotation ABOUT the bone — is free, and a child hinge's axis rides in this body's frame, so a free twist bends the child in an arbitrary plane. So: n_human = normalize(cross(human_parent_bone, human_child_bone)) the human's bend-plane normal a_hinge = rotate(swing, child_hinge_axis_local) where the hinge axis points now project both onto the plane ⟂ target_dir; twist = atan2 between them want = quatFromAxisAngle(target_dir, twist) · swing

6.3 The two-bone limb — exact, no solver

An arm is shoulder (2 DOF) + elbow (1 DOF) = 3 DOF, and a hand position is 3 numbers. It is exactly determined.

given shoulder S, hand target H, elbow hint E, lengths L₁ L₂ (the ROBOT's own): d = clamp(|H − S|, |L₁ − L₂| + ε, L₁ + L₂ − ε) cos(θ) = (L₁² + L₂² − d²) / (2 L₁ L₂) interior angle at the elbow flexion = π − θ departure from straight cos(α) = (L₁² + d² − L₂²) / (2 L₁ d) angle between upper bone and S→H n = normalize(cross(H − S, E − S)) the plane, FROM THE CAPTURE'S ELBOW upper = rotate(quatFromAxisAngle(n, α), normalize(H − S)) elbow = S + L₁ · upper

The plane comes from the capture's own elbow. Without it the elbow sits anywhere on a circle about the shoulder–hand axis — and that circle is the free-twist ambiguity that cost four experiments. The capture always knew the answer; it wasn't being asked.

6.4 Hinges: zero is not straight

measured on a bare model, no pipeline: qpos −17.2° → bend 92.3° 109.5 − 17.2 qpos −51.6° → bend 57.9° 109.5 − 51.6 qpos −80.2° → bend 29.3° 109.5 − 80.2 qpos +22.9° → bend 132.4° 109.5 + 22.9 bend = rest_bend + qpos, exactly. So: qpos = wanted_flexion − rest_flexion

Every flexion heuristic wrote the wanted bend straight into the coordinate. Writing −90.9 asked for 18.6 and got exactly that. Four turns of geometric explanations — cone relations, wrong bones, wrong joint indices — were all eliminated by measurement before the real cause appeared. A joint coordinate is an offset from the model's rest, never an absolute angle.

For completeness, a hinge whose axis is not perpendicular to its bone sweeps a cone, and the mapping is cos(φ) = cos²γ + sin²γ·cos(qpos) with maximum bend 2γ. humanoid.xml's elbow is perpendicular (γ = 90°), so this reduces to the identity here — but a real robot's hinges often are not, and hingeAngleForFlexion handles it.

6.5 The Jacobian and damped least squares

jacp[:, j] = cdof[j].lin + cross(cdof[j].ang, point − subtree_com[root]) jacr[:, j] = cdof[j].ang for each DOF j on the path body → world (no new physics; comPos made it) (JᵀWJ + λI) dq = JᵀW e SPD by construction, so a Cholesky solve qpos ← integratePos(qpos, dq) NEVER qpos += dq: a free joint's quaternion does not add

What to take from GMR, in order of value

  1. Warm start. One line. Directly addresses the measured jitter and the free-twist circle.
  2. Sequential task groups. Torso/pelvis/thighs first, then arms/feet/head. Removes weight tuning between limbs.
  3. Joint limits inside the step. Scale dq so no joint crosses its range.
  4. Per-limb scale. Only once the above are in.

9. Mistakes in the current code

Found by re-reading everything for this document. In order of consequence.

1. The example still resets to qpos0 every frame.

The comment says "so a bad frame cannot poison the next." It was the right call while the pipeline was untrustworthy; it is now the single largest measurable defect (25–37°/frame of excess motion). GMR never resets.

2. The rest_flexion fix is in the harness but not yet in the example.

The 33% hand-error improvement was measured on the harness. The example's poseRobot still writes the wanted bend straight into the coordinate. This is precisely the harness/example divergence the project spent four rounds closing — reopened by the newest fix. Wire rest_flexion through RetargetPose from refreshRobotMap.

3. Three mechanisms coexist in poseFromRetarget: twist offsets, aim_at_child, and two_bone.

Each has a flag; the interactions are not all measured. Two-bone is off because the hand didn't respond — which turned out to be the rest_flexion bug, now fixed. It should be re-measured, and then one of the three should go.

4. Position IK targets are built with one global scale.

1.315 / rest_pos[shoulder][1] — the shoulder-height ratio. It puts the shoulders right and everything else proportionally wrong. GMR's per-body scale table is the fix, but only after the mechanism is settled.

5. Dead weight that should be deleted, not kept "just in case".

robot_limb_correction, robot_rest_alignment, robot_human_reference, robot_axis_map, robot_desired_global, robot_identity_map, robot_identity_alignment, source_delta_z_up, the facing yaw, lafan_to_humanoid's rotation_weight on 0-DOF bodies (head, hand: their weights do nothing). Each was a mechanism papering over a bug in another; the bugs are fixed.

6. The scorecard's "upper arm POSITION" is measured relative to the torso but reported as stage 2.

It is a consequence of the torso's placement and the fixed shoulder offset, not an independent quantity. Relabel or drop; a stage that cannot move independently misleads.

7. Frame 320 got worse with the rest_flexion fix (0.190 → 0.424).

It is the frame with the longest wanted reach (0.460 of 0.624). Unexplained; the outlier to chase once two-bone is re-measured.

Fake numbers this project produced, all caught

  • A "rest check" of qmul(q, conj(q)) — identity for any q — read 0.0000 on device while proving nothing. Twice.
  • A Spine2/Spine3 mismatch between the ruler and the code.
  • A torso position of 0.913 m: world-absolute compared against root-relative.
  • A spine bone measured against a shoulder bone.
  • An "irreducible 0.21 m floor" derived on a system with three known defects; later beaten by half.
  • Numbers quoted from a harness running a different implementation from the example — four divergences.
  • A "no effect" conclusion from an edit that had silently not applied.

A wrong number sends work in a wrong direction, where wrong code merely fails. The harness needs the same suspicion as the code, and arguably more.

11. The lessons that generalise

Two reference poses must match in kind, not just in pose. "Both are rest poses" is not enough. Ask of both sides: what exactly does this quaternion measure, and is the other side measuring the same thing? Broken five ways here.
Zero is not straight. A joint coordinate is an offset from the model's rest configuration, never an absolute angle. Measure the rest bend once and subtract it.
Copy angles, never lengths. A robot's bone lengths are fixed. Asking it to match a human's joint positions asks for lengths it doesn't have, and a solver answers by bending a straight limb. Take the direction from the source and the magnitude from the model. Fixed the shoulder, the elbow, and the T-pose separately before it was recognised as one rule.
Draw the two things you are comparing, before comparing them. A carefully chosen scalar can be consistent with the wrong picture; a picture cannot. One drawing settled what a "hand at shoulder height" check had confirmed wrongly.
A target is not a pose. A robot body has no writable orientation; everything below the root moves only through joints. Several rounds of A/B testing compared formulas that reached exactly one body. Ask: can the thing I am varying actually reach the thing I am looking at?
Measure the link you changed, not the end of the chain. A single forearm number ranked the candidates backwards; the best formula looked worst because the forearm sits below both the shoulder and the elbow.
One implementation, measured and shipped. A harness that duplicates the code it measures is measuring a guess. Four divergences, every one invisible until a screenshot disagreed with a number.
Everything wrong in the same way is one global error, not many local ones. A per-joint framework cannot express a global constant, so the search never included "one number for all sixteen." The robot was wrong identically on every bone for many rounds before that was read correctly.
Minimal tests crack what pipeline prints cannot. fitBodyRotation, jacBody, solveTwoBoneLimb, and the hinge rest bend: each took one bare-model test after several in-pipeline prints had failed. Isolate the mechanism and ask the whole question at once.
A strong argument for deleting code is still not a measurement. Every rest-pose bug belonged to mechanism A and B was structurally immune — and A won by 21° when measured. Deleting would have traded a present capability for the absence of past problems.
Identical output means the change did not happen. A scripted edit whose anchor did not match left the file untouched, and the test produced numbers identical to the decimal. After any anchored edit, grep for the text just written.
Check whether the reference already solved it. flomo_to_geno_bvh.py contained the answer the whole time. It was not named "twist"; its docstring named the symptom. Grep the reference for the symptom.

12. The plan, revised

Ordered by expected value per unit of work, and by what the evidence supports rather than what the argument favours.

Step 1 — Close the divergence the newest fix reopened. (one turn)

Wire rest_flexion into the example's refreshRobotMap so the shipped pipeline gets the 33% hand improvement the harness measured. Then re-verify ARM FOCUS on the shared path. Until this is done, no number in this document describes the example.

Step 2 — Re-measure two-bone IK with rest_flexion in. (one turn)

It was switched off because the hand did not respond — which was the rest-bend bug, not the solve. The closed form is exact and tested; the prediction is hand error near zero on every in-reach frame. If it lands, delete aim_at_child for arms: one mechanism fewer.

Step 3 — Warm start. (one line, then measure stages 8 and 9)

Keep qpos between frames. The jitter metric exists precisely so this can be judged: it should cut 25–37°/frame sharply while leaving the static stages roughly alone. If it does not, the free-twist story is wrong.

Step 4 — Sequential task groups. (GMR's structure)

Group 1: torso, pelvis, thighs. Group 2: arms, feet, head. Solve each to convergence in turn. Removes the weight-tuning between limbs and directly targets the "helps arms, hurts legs" trade that ended two experiments.

Step 5 — Delete. (the pass this document makes possible)

Everything in §9 item 5. Each was a mechanism papering over a bug in another, and the bugs are fixed. The measurement infrastructure catches any regression the deletion causes.

Step 6 — Joint limits inside the step; per-limb scale. (tuning, last)

Only once the mechanism is settled. Tuning before that produced eleven candidate orientation formulas, of which none was the answer.

What not to do

  • Do not add a fourth orientation mechanism. Every extra one so far was papering over a bug in the others.
  • Do not tune weights before step 4. A priority scheme removes most of what weights arbitrate.
  • Do not quote a harness number as a property of the example without confirming they run the same code.
  • Do not conclude "no effect" from identical output. Grep for the edit first.

How to explain what we have done

Honestly: the retargeting mechanism is now correct in its targets and measured against the code that ships. The remaining error is a mix of a real model limit (a 2-DOF shoulder cannot both aim and twist; a 1-DOF elbow bends in one plane), a cold-start artefact with a known fix, and a few concrete unfinished items in §9. The legs are good (knee 6.6°, thigh 12.5°). The arms are approximate and improving (hand 0.24 m). The torso is respectable (35° A, 25° B).

The larger achievement is the instrumentation: a self-contained verification that has overturned the author's own judgement five times in five turns, on questions that would previously have been settled by argument and shipped. That is what makes the plan above executable without a screenshot after every step.

Every claim in this document traces to a measurement recorded in src/notes/retarget_recipe.md, src/notes/twist_port_plan.md, or src/notes/retarget_plan.md, or to a test in src/robot.zig and src/robot_mjcf.zig. Where a number is a prediction rather than a result, it is marked as one.
